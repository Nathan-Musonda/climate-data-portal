Which years were the most difficult for Zambia?
Shows a combined climate stress score for each year — combining temperature, rainfall deficit, drought severity, and flood frequency into a single view.
📅 How to read this: Taller bars mean more climate stress in that year — when temperatures were high, rainfall was low, drought was severe, and floods were frequent all at the same time. The years with the tallest bars are the ones that required the most emergency response and caused the most disruption to farming and daily life. If recent years have the tallest bars, it confirms that climate conditions are getting harder over time.
Which provinces face the most combined climate risk?
Ranks Zambia's provinces by their overall climate vulnerability — combining temperature, rainfall, drought, and flood data into one risk comparison.
🗺️ What this means: Provinces at the top of this chart face pressure from multiple climate directions at once — not just one problem but several overlapping stresses. These are the areas that most urgently need investment in drought-resistant farming, flood barriers, water storage, and early warning systems.
Does more rain always mean more floods?
Compares annual rainfall against flood event frequency — showing whether high-rainfall years consistently produce more flood events across Zambia.
🌧️ What to look for: In most years, more rainfall does produce more flood events — but not always. Sometimes moderate rainfall on already-saturated ground causes more flooding than very heavy rain falling on dry soil. Years where flood events are high but rainfall is only moderate are particularly important — they signal that ground conditions are already dangerously wet going into the rainy season.
Do dry years always produce the worst droughts?
Compares annual rainfall against drought severity — showing how directly low rainfall translates into drought conditions across Zambia.
🌵 What to look for: Years where the rainfall bar is short and the drought severity is high confirm the direct link between low rainfall and drought. However, some years show moderate rainfall but still high drought — this happens when rainfall is poorly timed (arriving too late in the season) or when temperatures are high enough to evaporate moisture before crops can use it.
Which months carry the most climate risk?
Shows how climate stress varies across the months of the year — combining temperature, rainfall, and flood risk into a seasonal vulnerability view.
📆 Zambia's risk calendar: November to March is the rainy season — high rainfall and flood risk. April to June is the transition — drought risk builds as rains end. July to October is the dry season — peak drought and heat stress. Understanding which months carry what risk helps communities and farmers plan ahead: when to store water, when to plant, and when to prepare for possible floods or dry spells.

🌍 The bigger picture — what four variables tell us together

Looking at temperature, rainfall, floods, and drought together reveals something that looking at each one alone cannot: Zambia is experiencing a pattern of compounding climate stress. Hotter temperatures cause faster evaporation — making dry years feel even drier. Low rainfall leads to drought — but when rains finally arrive after a drought, the dry cracked ground cannot absorb water fast enough, causing localised flooding. This cycle — drought followed by flash flooding — is becoming more common. The provinces that suffer most are those caught in the middle: too dry for reliable farming, but occasionally hit by intense short bursts of rainfall that cause damage rather than relief. This page helps identify exactly where and when these compounding risks are highest, so that resources and planning can be directed where they are needed most.

About this page

This page combines data from the Temperature, Rainfall, Flood Events, and Drought sections of the portal. All data covers Zambia from 2015 to 2025, sourced from the Zambia Meteorological Department (ZMD), NASA Earth Observations, and the SADC Climate Services Centre.

The combined stress score and province risk index are calculated from the four climate variables available in this portal. For deeper analysis of any individual variable, use the dedicated pages in the navigation menu.
